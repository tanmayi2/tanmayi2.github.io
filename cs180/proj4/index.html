project 4

stitching photo mosaics

Project 4 is about stitching photos into mosaics. In Part A, I pick correspondences between photos taken from one center of projection by hand and recover the homography with least squares. This homography allows us to inverse-warp one image into the other's plane, and blend the two together to create a panorama effect. In Part B, I replace the hand-clicked correspondences with features that are detected, described, and matched automatically.

Part A: Image Warping and Mosaicing

A.1: Shoot the Pictures

All photos were taken on my phone, two sets from a rooftop and one from the sidewalk. I kept the phone in one spot and rotated it between shots so that each pair is related by a homography, with roughly half of the frame overlapping.

A.2: Recover Homographies

I labeled 8 correspondences per pair by hand with the course correspondence tool and saved them as JSON. Window corners and the corners of the rooftop shed gave the most reliable points.

computeH takes im1_pts and im2_pts, two nx2 matrices holding the clicked points (xi, yi) in image 1 and (ui, vi) in image 2. H maps (x, y) → (u, v) through homogeneous coordinates: multiplying [x, y, 1] by H gives [u′, v′, w′], and dividing by w′ gives u = u′/w′ and v = v′/w′. Fixing the bottom-right entry of H to 1 leaves 8 unknowns h1 … h8.

Multiplying u = u′/w′ and v = v′/w′ through by w′ and moving everything that depends on h to the left gives h1x + h2y + h3 − u·h7x − u·h8y = u and h4x + h5y + h6 − v·h7x − v·h8y = v. Both are linear in the unknowns, so each correspondence i contributes a 2x8 block Ai and a 2x1 block bi. Stacking all n of them gives the system Ah = b.

With 4 points A is square and the system solves exactly; with more it is overdetermined, so computeH solves it in the least-squares sense and reshapes [h1 … h8, 1] into the 3x3 matrix H.

The main thing I took away from this part was how much simpler and faster it is to project many points at once, stacking them into a matrix and multiplying by H in one shot instead of looping, which I leaned on heavily for the warps below.

A.3: Warp the Images

I implemented two different warps, warpImageNearestNeighbor and warpImageBilinear. Both warps use inverse mapping so the output has no holes. I push the four corners of the input through H to get the output bounding box, build a grid of every output pixel (u, v, 1), multiply by H−1, and divide by the third coordinate to get source coordinates (x, y). Only pixels whose (x, y) lands inside the input image are filled so that everything else stays black.

Nearest neighbor (warpImageNearestNeighbor) rounds (x, y) to the closest pixel and copies it. Bilinear (warpImageBilinear) takes the 2x2 neighborhood at x, y and averages the four pixels.

Rectification. To test the warp on a single image, I clicked the four corners of a rectangular object and mapped them to a hand-chosen rectangle. The output canvas is the bounding box of the whole warped photo, so the wall around the target gets warped along with it. The order of the target corners matters: pairing the clicked corners with the square in a different order flips or rotates the rectified result, so the same code can flip an image just by reordering four coordinates.

Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear. At full size the two look the same. But when you zoom in, you can tell the difference near the edges. Nearest neighbor keeps hard pixel edges along the poster corner and the painting's border, while bilinear smooths them out a bit more.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I keep image 2 fixed and warp image 1 into its plane. Image 1 is warped with both the homography matrix H and a translation T and image 2 is pasted at an offset.

Getting the overlap took many tries. My first version simply placed the warped image 1 on top of image 2, which leaves a strong edge wherever the two images disagree. The second averaged the overlapping pixels equally: both images are now visible in the overlap, but the boundary is still a hard line and any misaligned detail shows up twice. What finally worked was radial weighting. Each image gets a weight that is 1 at its center and falls off linearly to 0 at its edges; for image 1 the weight is evaluated in its original coordinates by reusing the inverse-mapped (x, y) grid. The mosaic is the weighted average (w1I1 + w2I2) / (w1 + w2) over the whole canvas, which reduces to a plain copy wherever only one image has weight. I believe an edge-based scheme, weighting by the distance to the nearest image edge instead of the distance from the center, should work as well.

Where the correspondences are placed decides where the two images line up. For mosaic 2 I picked 20 points and deliberately put almost all of them on and around the large tree, the focal point of the scene, so that it would align as well as possible. It does: the tree and the roofline behind it are sharp. The cost is that fine detail with no correspondences nearby, like the bushes and grass near the bottom of the frame, is only aligned as well as the homography extrapolates there, and it shows up doubled.

Mosaic 3 was the hardest to blend, and I think the photos are to blame: I most likely shifted the center of projection slightly between the two shots, so no single homography fits both. This shows up in the fit itself. The largest residual of the least-squares system, max |Ah − b|, is about 60 px for this set, compared with 8 px for mosaic 2 and 4 px for mosaic 1, even with 24 correspondences. This resulted in ghosting on the trees and the light pole in the overlap.

Part B: Feature Matching for Autostitching

Part B replaces the hand-clicked correspondences from Part A with features that are found and matched automatically. I followed a simplified version of the paper “Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches” by Brown et al.

B.1: Harris Corner Detection

I used the provided harris.py, which runs a Harris point detector. On its own it returns around 30,000 corners per image, which is far too many to describe and match.

Adaptive non-maximal suppression (ANMS) picks a max number of 500 points that are both strong and spread out. For each corner i, I compute a suppression radius ri: the distance to the closest corner j that is sufficiently stronger, meaning f(xi) < crobust · f(xj) with crobust = 0.9. The result is 500 corners that cover the image evenly instead of piling up on the most textured region.

B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

For each ANMS corner I take the 40x40 window around it in the grayscale image, blur it with a Gaussian (σ = 2), and sample every 5th pixel to get a 8x8 patch. Sampling from the larger blurred window is what makes the descriptor forgiving of small misalignments since an 8x8 crop at full resolution would be far too sensitive. Each patch is normalized by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation and then flattened into a 1x64 vector.

B.3: Feature Matching

To match features I compute the distance between every descriptor in image 1 and every descriptor in image 2 (using dist2), and for each feature in image 1 find its first and second nearest neighbors in image 2. Following Lowe, I keep a match only if the ratio of the first to the second nearest-neighbor distance is below 0.6. The idea witht this is that a real match should be much closer than any other candidate, so a ratio near 1 means the feature is ambiguous (since the first and second are too similar). Out of 500 features per image, the ratio test leaves 169 matches for set 1, 163 for set 2, and 109 for set 3.

B.4: RANSAC for Robust Homography

Even after the ratio test some matches are wrong, and because least squares minimizes squared error, a single bad match can really effect the homography. So I fit H with 4-point RANSAC. For 500 iterations I pick 4 random matches, compute H exactly from them with computeH from Part A, project every matched point from image 1 into image 2, and count how many land within 4 pixels of their match. I keep the H with the largest inlier set. RANSAC kept 95 of 169 matches for set 1, 108 of 163 for set 2, and 71 of 109 for set 3.

Finally I pass the RANSAC homography to the same mosaic code from Part A. Below, each manual mosaic from Part A is on the left and the automatic one is on the right. For set 1 the ghosting has really reduced in the automatic feature matching version. For set 2, the automatic result is pretty similar. Set 3 is best improvement, its automatic mosaic is much cleaner than my manual one, with less doubling on the light pole and the trees.
