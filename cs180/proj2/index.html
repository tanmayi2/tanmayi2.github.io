Part 2.1: Image "Sharpening"

Now let's "sharpen" an image! To do so, we will simply add some of the higher frequencies in an image (the taj mahal) to itself. How do we get the high frequencies? By simply subtracting the low frequencies (the result of the 2d gaussian blur filter). Some hyperparameters to play with here are how much gaussian blur to use (in terms of kernel size and standard deviation), as well as how much high frequency to add back in. Below is a sharpened taj mahal in grayscale with a lot of high frequency added.

Part 2.2: Hybrid Images

What else can we do when we separate frequencies? The SIGGRAPH 2006 paper by Oliva, Torralba, and Schyns, Hybrid Images, introduced a fun effect you can create that takes advantage of the human eye. Essentially you take the high frequencies from one image and superimpose them onto the low frequencies of another. When looking at it from far away, the low frequency image appears, and when looking at it from close up, the high frequency image does instead.

One of my images is a blooming peony across time where we have a half-opened peony as the low frequency image and a fully opened peony as the high frequency image. I had to play around with the hyperparameters which respectively control the gaussian kernel size (I chose 17) and the interpolation between both images to get it to look its best.

Part 2.3 and 2.4: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks and Multiresolution Blending

What if we could blend images together in a (semi)-natural manner? To make the oraple (blended orange and apple) come alive, I implemented the following gaussian and laplacian stacks.

Together, they create the oraple!

I also recreated the figure from the paper demonstrating the laplacian stack.

What makes this work is that each frequency band gets a transition matched to its level of detail. Fine detail is blended across a narrow seam and coarse features are blended across a wide seam, so the jump from the darker apple to the lighter orange becomes a gradual gradient rather than a visible line. If you used a single mask for everything, you'd have to compromise: a sharp mask leaves an obvious seam, and a blurry mask makes the fine textures overlap.

I further tried the vertical seam trick on a day to night NYC image, where the left side of the image is daytime and the right side of the image is nighttime.

Lastly, I tried blending an orange slice into a bicycle wheel. This required the use of a circular mask.
